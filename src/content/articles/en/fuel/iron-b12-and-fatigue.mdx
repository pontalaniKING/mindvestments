If you've already fixed the sleep basics — consistent wake time, morning light, caffeine cut-off — and fatigue is still a persistent problem, ask specifically for a ferritin test and a serum B12 test, not just a routine blood count. Both are common, both are easy to miss on standard screening, and both are straightforward to correct once identified.

## Why this works

Iron and B12 deficiency are two of the more common, genuinely fixable causes of persistent tiredness, and both are easy to miss for the same reason: a standard complete blood count doesn't reliably catch either one on its own.

Iron deficiency without anaemia — low iron stores that haven't yet dropped haemoglobin below the threshold that shows up as anaemia on a basic blood count — is common and still produces fatigue. It's a particular issue for menstruating women, where estimates put iron deficiency prevalence in the range of 9–11%, roughly double the rate in men. Because early iron deficiency doesn't always show up as anaemia, ferritin — a direct marker of iron stores — is the specific test needed, not just a haemoglobin reading.

B12 deficiency has a similar blind spot. It classically causes macrocytic anaemia — enlarged red blood cells that a standard blood count would flag — but a meaningful share of people with confirmed B12 deficiency don't show this pattern. In one clinical study, over 40% of people with confirmed deficiency had a normal, not enlarged, red cell size, meaning a standard blood count looked entirely unremarkable despite genuine deficiency. Fatigue was the single most common symptom reported, alongside neurological signs like tingling and numbness that are easy to attribute to something else entirely. B12 deficiency is more common in people eating little or no animal food, older adults with reduced absorption, and anyone on long-term metformin or acid-reducing medication, which interfere with B12 absorption.

## How to do it

1. If fatigue persists after the sleep fundamentals are genuinely addressed, ask your doctor specifically for a ferritin test and a serum B12 test — say the names, since a routine blood count alone often won't catch either.
2. Mention any relevant risk factors: heavy periods, a plant-based or low-meat diet, long-term use of metformin or proton pump inhibitors, or a history of gastrointestinal surgery or malabsorption.
3. If a deficiency is confirmed, treatment is usually straightforward — iron supplementation for iron deficiency, B12 supplementation (oral or injectable, depending on the cause and severity) for B12 deficiency — but should be guided by your doctor's assessment of the underlying cause, not self-directed.
4. Don't start iron supplements speculatively. Iron is not like most vitamins — the body has no efficient way to get rid of excess, and unnecessary long-term supplementation can cause harm.
5. If levels come back normal, treat that as genuinely useful information rather than a dead end — it rules out two common, specific causes and points you toward other explanations worth investigating.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a normal general blood count rules out both.** It reliably catches neither early iron deficiency nor a meaningful share of B12 deficiency cases — the specific tests matter.
- **Starting an iron supplement without testing because "it can't hurt."** It can, in people who aren't actually deficient, through gradual iron accumulation over time.
- **Not mentioning diet or medication history to your doctor.** Risk factors like a plant-based diet or long-term metformin use meaningfully change how likely a deficiency is, and are useful context for interpreting results.
- **Attributing fatigue immediately to one of these without testing.** Fatigue has many common causes — this is one specific, testable possibility worth ruling in or out, not an assumption to act on unconfirmed.
- **Stopping supplementation as soon as you feel slightly better.** Correcting a genuine deficiency, especially iron, typically takes longer than symptoms take to start improving — follow your doctor's guidance on duration and retesting.

## What should change

If a genuine deficiency is identified and corrected, meaningful improvement in energy typically shows up within four to eight weeks, tracking with the levels themselves recovering rather than happening overnight. If levels are normal, that's useful information pointing you toward other causes rather than a result to be disappointed by.

## Your next step

If the sleep fundamentals haven't actually been addressed yet — consistent wake time, morning light, an earlier caffeine cut-off — those are worth ruling in or out first, since they're free, fast to test, and are the more common cause of persistent daytime fatigue.
