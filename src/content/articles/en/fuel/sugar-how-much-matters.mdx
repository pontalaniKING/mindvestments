If you're going to focus your effort anywhere on sugar, focus it on sugar-sweetened drinks specifically — soda, sweetened juice drinks, sweetened coffee and tea — rather than treating all dietary sugar as an equally urgent problem. The evidence against sugar isn't uniform across how it's consumed, and most of the strongest findings are about the liquid form specifically.

## Why this works

The World Health Organization recommends keeping added sugar under 10% of daily calories, with a conditional recommendation to aim under 5% for extra benefit — mainly for dental health, where the evidence is genuinely strong and consistent. On weight and metabolic disease, the picture is more nuanced than the "sugar is sugar" framing usually presented.

A detailed evidence review examining this specifically found something worth knowing: most of the human research linking high sugar intake to obesity and metabolic disease comes from studies of sugar-sweetened beverages, and studies looking specifically at sugar consumed in solid food have often found null or much weaker results. This isn't a loophole or a technicality — there's a real, plausible mechanism behind it. Sugar dissolved in a drink has no fibre slowing its absorption, delivers a fast, large glucose load, and — critically — doesn't trigger the same fullness signals that solid food does, so people tend not to compensate by eating less elsewhere. A can of soda and an equivalent amount of sugar eaten as part of a meal with protein, fat, and fibre are, metabolically, not the same experiment, even though both get counted identically as "grams of sugar" on a label.

This doesn't mean solid-food sugar is irrelevant or that unlimited sweets are fine — heavily sweetened solid foods are very often also low in fibre and easy to overeat for the same reasons covered in the article on ultra-processed food, and dental health specifically doesn't care whether the sugar was liquid or solid. But the size and consistency of the evidence specifically implicating sugary drinks is considerably stronger than the evidence against sugar in food generally, which is exactly the distinction most sugar-focused advice skips over.

## How to do it

1. Treat sugar-sweetened drinks as the priority cut, not sugar generally — this is where the strongest, most consistent evidence of harm sits.
2. Replace with water, unsweetened tea or coffee, or sparkling water rather than diet versions as a permanent habit — diet drinks are a reasonable transition tool but plain alternatives are the more durable default.
3. Don't panic about a moderate amount of sugar in solid food, particularly food that also contains fibre, protein, or fat, which blunts the same absorption spike that makes liquid sugar more concerning.
4. For dental health specifically, frequency matters more than total amount — sipping a sugary drink over hours does more damage than the same amount consumed at once with a meal.
5. Read labels for sugar content in things that don't taste sweet — sauces, bread, flavoured yoghurt — since this is where sugar intake most often sneaks past people's attention, regardless of the liquid-versus-solid distinction.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a piece of fruit and a can of soda as equivalent because both contain sugar.** The fibre, water content, and lack of concentration in fruit change the metabolic response meaningfully — this is also why the evidence explicitly excludes fruit and vegetables from the sugars of concern.
- **Obsessing over sugar in meals while drinking sugary beverages daily.** This focuses effort on the weaker part of the evidence while leaving the stronger part unaddressed.
- **Assuming "no evidence against solid sugar" means "no limit needed."** The evidence is weaker and less consistent, not absent — moderate is still the reasonable target, just without the alarm that applies to sugary drinks.
- **Switching to large amounts of diet soda as a long-term solution without examining why the habit exists.** It's a reasonable bridge, but plain water or unsweetened options are the more sustainable long-term default.
- **Ignoring dental health in this calculation.** Cavities don't care about the liquid-versus-solid distinction the metabolic evidence draws — frequent sugar exposure of any kind is the relevant variable there.

## What should change

Cutting sugary drinks specifically tends to show up in easier weight maintenance and steadier energy within a matter of weeks, since it removes a genuinely large, easy-to-underestimate source of quickly absorbed calories. Broader metabolic markers, where they improve, build over a longer timescale and are harder to attribute to this one change alone if other things are shifting at the same time.
