Set your phone to deliver notifications in three scheduled batches a day — something like morning, mid-afternoon, and evening — rather than continuously as they arrive, and rather than turning them off entirely. The middle option, tested directly against both extremes, is the one that actually worked.

## Why this works

Notifications are deliberately built around unpredictable timing, and that's not incidental — a randomised, controlled field experiment on this explicitly describes standard notification delivery as exploiting the same psychological mechanism as a slot machine: rewards (a message, a like, news) arriving on an unpredictable schedule, which is a far stronger driver of compulsive checking than rewards arriving on a predictable one. This is a well-established principle in behavioural psychology, not a loose metaphor.

The obvious fix people reach for is turning notifications off entirely, and this is where the research gets genuinely useful. In that same randomised experiment, comparing normal notifications, notifications batched into three fixed times a day, and no notifications at all, batching produced the best outcomes — lower stress, higher wellbeing. Turning notifications off completely did not win; it produced more anxiety and a stronger fear of missing out than either of the other two conditions. Removing the unpredictable rewards helped. Removing all information entirely introduced a different kind of stress.

A separate, more recent trial adds an important caveat: simply disabling notifications for a week didn't actually reduce how often people checked their phones or their total screen time — the checking habit persisted on its own, running independently of whether notifications were prompting it. It did modestly reduce how much people felt controlled by the habit, which suggests notifications and compulsive checking are related but partly separate problems, and fixing one doesn't automatically fix the other.

## How to do it

1. Use your phone's built-in scheduled or focus-mode notification settings to deliver most notifications in two or three set windows a day — morning, afternoon, evening is a reasonable default.
2. Keep genuinely time-sensitive alerts — calls from specific people, calendar reminders — outside the batching if that matters for your situation, rather than batching absolutely everything.
3. Don't aim for zero notifications as the end goal. The evidence specifically found that extreme underperforms the middle option.
4. If checking your phone has become a habit independent of actual notifications, address that separately — closing an app or moving it off your home screen does more for compulsive checking than notification settings alone.
5. Give it a week or two before judging — the shift from unpredictable to scheduled delivery takes a little adjustment, similar to any change in an established pattern.

## Common mistakes

- **Going straight to "do not disturb" or notifications fully off.** The direct experimental evidence found this backfires relative to batching, producing more anxiety, not less.
- **Assuming disabling notifications will also fix compulsive phone checking.** The more recent research found these are separate problems — checking habits often persist even once the notifications themselves stop.
- **Batching notifications but still checking the phone constantly out of habit.** The scheduling change addresses the external trigger; the habit itself may need its own approach.
- **Treating this as an all-or-nothing decision for every app.** Genuinely time-sensitive channels can reasonably stay outside the batching system.
- **Expecting an instant, dramatic shift.** The benefit is a steadier background state, not an overnight transformation.

## What should change

Expect measurably lower stress and a reduced sense of being constantly interrupted within the first few days of batching. If checking your phone out of habit persists despite fewer notifications arriving, that's a separate pattern worth its own attention — closing the specific apps involved tends to matter more there than notification timing.

## Your next step

If the deeper issue is that a switch away from your phone leaves you feeling scattered for several minutes afterward, that's attention residue rather than a notification problem specifically — a closure habit before switching addresses that more directly than changing notification settings does.
