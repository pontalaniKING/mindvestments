Don't treat "believing intelligence can grow" as a strategy on its own — the large-scale evidence says that belief shift, by itself, does remarkably little for most people. If a growth mindset approach is going to help, it needs to be paired with a concrete plan for what changes, and it tends to matter most for people who are genuinely struggling, not for those already doing fine.

## Why this works, or mostly doesn't

Growth mindset — the idea that believing your abilities can develop through effort leads to better resilience and achievement — is one of the most culturally successful psychological ideas of the past twenty years, shaping school curricula, corporate training programmes, and a huge share of everyday self-improvement advice. The compelling, memorable framing arrived well before the large-scale evidence did.

When that evidence finally arrived, it was considerably less impressive than the cultural reach suggested. A major 2018 meta-analysis spanning dozens of studies and thousands of participants found the overall effect of growth mindset interventions on academic achievement was d = 0.08 — in plain terms, enough to move an average student from roughly the 50th to the 53rd percentile, a genuinely negligible shift. A later, independent 2022 meta-analysis found an even smaller effect, d = 0.05, which became statistically non-significant once the analysis adjusted for publication bias — the tendency for positive results to get published and studied more than null ones. When researchers restricted analysis to only the highest-quality studies — pre-registered, properly blinded, with checks that the intervention actually changed people's stated beliefs — the effect on real-world outcomes disappeared entirely. Several attempted replications of Dweck's original foundational studies on praise and effort also failed to reproduce the original findings.

The story isn't a total debunking, though, and the nuance matters. A large, well-designed 2019 study published in Nature — specifically addressing the earlier failures by testing at national scale with careful measurement — found that a brief, low-cost mindset intervention did produce a real, if modest, improvement in grades specifically among lower-achieving students, and specifically in school environments where the surrounding culture reinforced the idea (teachers and peers who supported a growth-oriented approach rather than one enthusiastic poster in an otherwise unchanged classroom). Context, in other words, turned out to matter enormously — the same intervention that does almost nothing for an already-thriving student in a status-quo environment can produce a small, real benefit for a struggling student somewhere the surrounding culture actually reinforces it.

## How to do it

1. Don't expect a mindset shift alone — "I believe I can improve" — to meaningfully change outcomes for someone who's already doing reasonably well. The research specifically doesn't support that use case.
2. If you're genuinely struggling with something specific, pairing a growth-oriented belief with a concrete, structured plan (worth combining with an implementation intention, covered elsewhere) is more likely to actually help than the belief shift alone.
3. Environment matters more than the individual belief-change exercise itself — a supportive surrounding culture that reinforces effort and improvement did the heavy lifting in the one large study that found a real effect.
4. Be skeptical of growth mindset framed as a general life philosophy that alone explains success or failure — the evidence doesn't support that broad a claim, however intuitively appealing it is.
5. If you're in a position to influence a learning or work environment (as a teacher, manager, or parent), the research suggests structural and cultural support matters more than a one-off talk or poster about believing in yourself.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating growth mindset as a proven, general-purpose performance booster.** The average effect size across large, high-quality studies is close to zero for most people in most contexts.
- **Assuming the concept is entirely fake because the average effect is small.** The 2019 national study found a real, replicated benefit for a specific group in a specific context — dismissing it entirely overcorrects past what the evidence actually shows.
- **Applying a brief intervention (a talk, a poster, a single lesson) and expecting it to work regardless of the surrounding environment.** Context was the single biggest factor separating the studies that found real effects from those that didn't.
- **Using "just believe you can improve" as a substitute for an actual plan.** The evidence consistently shows belief change without a concrete behavioural plan does little on its own.
- **Ignoring the replication failures because the original framing was compelling.** Compelling communication and strong evidence are different things, and this is one of the clearer cases in recent psychology where they diverged for years before the evidence caught up.

## What should change

Don't expect measurable change from adopting growth-mindset language alone. If you pair a mindset shift with a concrete plan, particularly around something you're genuinely struggling with, and you're in a supportive environment, modest real improvement is plausible based on the best current evidence — but the mindset belief is doing far less of the work than popular framing suggests.
