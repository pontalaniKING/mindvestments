Alternate running and walking for the first few weeks rather than trying to run continuously from day one, and increase your total time by no more than about 10% a week. Most of what makes early running fail isn't a lack of willpower — it's a training load that increased faster than tendons and bones could adapt to.

## Why this works

Muscles adapt to new load within days. Tendons, ligaments, and bone take considerably longer — often weeks — which is the actual reason novice runners get injured at a notably higher rate than experienced ones: not because running itself is risky, but because a new runner's cardiovascular fitness usually improves faster than their connective tissue can keep up, so it becomes possible to run further and harder than the joints and tendons are ready for.

Cohort studies of new runners consistently find the injury rate is driven far more by how quickly weekly volume increases than by the running itself. The commonly cited "increase mileage by no more than 10% a week" guideline doesn't come from a single definitive trial — it's a reasonable, widely used approximation rather than a precisely proven threshold — but the underlying principle it represents, gradual rather than steep load increases, is well supported across the injury-prevention literature.

Run-walk intervals work particularly well for absolute beginners because they let cardiovascular effort rise gradually while capping the continuous impact load on tendons and joints in any single session — you get fitter before you're running far enough for the tissue adaptation to become the limiting factor.

## How to do it

1. Start with intervals: something like one minute running, two minutes walking, repeated for 20–25 minutes total, three times a week.
2. Extend the running portions gradually every week or two — run 90 seconds instead of 60, then two minutes, rather than jumping straight to continuous running.
3. Increase total session time by roughly 10% a week at most, not the running-to-walking ratio and the total time simultaneously.
4. Leave at least one full rest or easy day between running sessions in the first several weeks — this is where the tissue adaptation from the previous session actually catches up.
5. Judge progress by how you feel the day after, not just during the run itself. Lingering soreness or a specific sore spot the next day is a signal to hold the current level rather than progress.

## Common mistakes

- **Running continuously from the first session because walking breaks feel like giving up.** The walking breaks are the strategy, not a failure of it.
- **Increasing both distance and pace at the same time.** Progress one variable at a time so you can tell what's driving how you feel.
- **Chasing a specific 5k time early on.** Time goals encourage exactly the rapid load increases most likely to cause an injury that stops the whole project.
- **Ignoring specific, localised pain, as opposed to general fatigue.** General tiredness in the legs is normal. A specific sore spot that doesn't ease with rest is not.
- **Running the same route or surface every time out of habit.** Some variation in surface reduces repetitive load on exactly the same structures each session.

## What should change

Most beginners following a gradual run-walk progression can run continuously for 20–30 minutes within six to eight weeks, with soreness limited to normal post-exercise fatigue rather than anything sharp or localised. If progress stalls or pain appears at a consistent spot, that's a sign to hold the current level or scale back, not push through it.

## Your next step

Once continuous running feels comfortable, the next-highest-value change usually isn't running further — it's the two-sessions-a-week strength routine covered elsewhere, which measurably reduces running injury rates in several trials.
