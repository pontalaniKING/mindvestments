Leave at least a full day between strength sessions that hit the same muscles, and build in a deliberately lighter week roughly every four to six weeks. The workout is the stimulus; the adaptation you're actually training for happens afterward, during recovery — skip that part and you're not getting extra progress, you're interrupting the process that produces it.

## Why this works

A strength training session doesn't make you stronger by itself — it creates microscopic damage in muscle fibres and a temporary dip in performance capacity. The rebuilding process that follows, where the body repairs that tissue slightly stronger than before, is where the actual strength gain comes from, and it takes roughly 24 to 48 hours to meaningfully progress for a given muscle group after a real training stimulus. Train the same muscles again before that process has run its course and you're adding more damage on top of incomplete repair, not stacking additional gains.

The evidence for planned lighter weeks, often called deload weeks, is separate from the day-to-day recovery point but tells a similar story at a longer timescale. Research comparing structured training with periodic lighter weeks against training hard continuously generally finds the periodised approach produces equal or better long-term strength gains, with meaningfully lower rates of overreaching and injury. Continuous hard training without planned lighter periods tends to produce a slow accumulation of fatigue that eventually shows up as stalled progress, nagging joint pain, or disrupted sleep — a pattern sports medicine describes as overreaching, which left unaddressed can progress to a much harder to reverse state of overtraining.

## How to do it

1. If you're following the two-sessions-a-week beginner approach, the days off are already built in — resist the urge to add extra sessions once it starts feeling easy.
2. For anyone training more often, avoid working the same muscle group on back-to-back days; alternate which patterns you train if you want to train most days.
3. Roughly every four to six weeks, take a lighter week: same movements, meaningfully reduced weight or effort, rather than skipping training entirely.
4. Watch for early signals that a lighter week is due before waiting for a stall: persistent soreness that doesn't resolve, sleep feeling worse, or motivation dropping despite training going fine on paper.
5. Sleep is doing a large share of the actual recovery work — a hard training block on consistently poor sleep gets meaningfully less benefit from the same sessions.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating rest days as a sign of insufficient effort.** They're the mechanism, not the absence of one.
- **Training through nagging joint or tendon pain because "it's not a real injury yet."** This is usually the early, still-reversible stage of the exact problem a deload week is meant to prevent.
- **Never programming a lighter week because progress currently feels fine.** The lighter week is preventative, not a response to a problem that's already visible.
- **Confusing a rest day with total inactivity.** A walk or light stretching on a rest day doesn't interfere with recovery and often helps circulation and mood.
- **Judging a deload week's usefulness by how the week itself feels.** Its value shows up in the weeks after, not during.

## What should change

Expect steadier week-to-week progress over two to three months when rest days and periodic lighter weeks are built in, compared with training hard continuously — the difference shows up as fewer stalls and fewer nagging issues rather than as a single dramatic change.

## Your next step

If a specific joint or tendon has been quietly bothering you for more than a couple of weeks despite rest days, that's past the point a deload alone reliably fixes — worth a physiotherapist's opinion before it becomes a longer interruption.
