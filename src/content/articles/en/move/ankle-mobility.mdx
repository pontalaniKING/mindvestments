Three minutes, most days: stand facing a wall, feet a comfortable distance back, and rock your front knee forward over your toes without lifting your heel, feeling a stretch build in the calf and ankle. This unglamorous joint quietly limits far more than people realise.

## Why this works

Ankle dorsiflexion — the ability of your shin to travel forward over your foot without the heel lifting — is one of those things nobody thinks about until it's limiting them, and it turns out to limit quite a lot. Research on squat mechanics finds ankle dorsiflexion range is one of the strongest predictors of how deep someone can squat with good form, alongside hip mobility — restricted ankle range doesn't just stop you going deeper, it also tends to push the knee inward and shift load onto the lower back and knees to compensate.

The same restriction matters well past the gym. Everyday movements — a full squat pattern, descending stairs, getting up from a low chair — require a baseline amount of ankle dorsiflexion, generally cited around 10 degrees, with more demanding movements like running needing considerably more. As ankle range narrows with age and inactivity, research in older adults links it directly to impaired balance, altered gait, and higher fall risk — restricted dorsiflexion is specifically associated with reduced ground clearance during walking, meaning a foot is more likely to catch on an uneven surface or a small obstacle.

The genuinely good news is that ankle mobility responds to simple, direct work faster and more reliably than many other joints, largely because the limiting factor is usually a straightforwardly stretchable structure — the calf muscles and Achilles tendon — rather than something more structurally complex.

## How to do it

1. Stand facing a wall, one foot back about a comfortable stride's length.
2. Keeping your back heel flat on the ground, bend your front knee toward the wall, letting your back ankle bend to allow it.
3. Hold the deepest comfortable position for a few seconds, then release — repeat for 8–10 reps per side, or hold a static stretch for 30–45 seconds per side.
4. For a weighted, more functional version, hold a light dumbbell or kettlebell at your chest and perform slow, controlled ankle rocks in a split stance, which trains the range under a bit of load rather than passively.
5. Pair this with the stiff-hips routine on days you do both — squatting depth is usually limited by ankle and hip range together, not just one or the other.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting the back heel lift during the stretch.** This is the single most common error, and it removes most of the actual ankle stretch, shifting the work elsewhere.
- **Only stretching, never loading the new range.** A few reps of bodyweight squats or split-stance work after stretching helps the nervous system actually use the range you just gained.
- **Ignoring it because squats aren't part of your routine.** Stairs, getting off the floor, and general balance all depend on the same range regardless of whether you train formally.
- **Expecting an overnight fix for a genuinely bony restriction.** Some ankle stiffness is structural rather than soft-tissue based, particularly after an old sprain — if stretching produces no change over several weeks, that's worth a physiotherapist's assessment rather than more stretching.
- **Skipping it in favour of "bigger" mobility work.** Hips and shoulders get most of the attention in general mobility advice; ankles are just as functionally important and get a fraction of the focus.

## What should change

Expect a noticeably deeper, more comfortable squat position within two to four weeks of consistent work, along with less compensatory knee cave-in if that's been a pattern for you. Balance and fall-risk benefits build over a longer timescale and matter most as a preventative measure well before they'd be noticeable day to day.

## Your next step

If squat depth is still limited after ankle mobility improves, hip mobility is usually the next thing to check — the two work together, and the stiff-hips routine covers the other half of the equation.
