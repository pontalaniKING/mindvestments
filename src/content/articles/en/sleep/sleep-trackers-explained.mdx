Use your sleep tracker for the trend over weeks — is your average sleep getting longer or shorter, more or less consistent — rather than treating each night's score as a verdict. This is less about whether the device is "accurate" and more about what it's actually accurate at.

## Why this works

Consumer sleep trackers are genuinely decent at one specific thing: telling whether you were asleep or awake, minute by minute, based on movement and heart rate. Validation studies comparing them against polysomnography, the clinical gold standard, generally find 85–95% agreement on basic sleep-versus-wake classification. That's good enough to be useful for tracking overall patterns like total sleep time and consistency.

Where they get much shakier is everything past that basic classification. The same validation research consistently finds trackers overestimate how much of the night you were actually asleep and underestimate how much time you spent briefly awake — in one 2025 comparison against clinical recordings, devices overestimated sleep efficiency by 2% to as much as 10%, and underestimated waking time by up to 40%. Sleep stage breakdowns — the light, deep, and REM percentages every app now displays prominently — are built from indirect signals like movement and heart rate variability rather than the brainwave data polysomnography actually uses, and different devices frequently disagree with each other on the same night, which is a strong hint that none of them should be treated as precise.

There's also a cost to over-trusting the data that has nothing to do with accuracy. Sleep researchers coined the term orthosomnia to describe a specific, growing pattern: people who feel objectively fine but become convinced something is wrong with their sleep because an app told them so, and who then develop genuine anxiety and insomnia chasing a perfect score. The device intended to help can end up creating the exact problem it was bought to solve.

## How to do it

1. Look at your tracker weekly or monthly, not nightly. Total sleep time and bedtime consistency over two to four weeks are the metrics with the most evidence behind actually mattering.
2. Treat the sleep score and stage percentages as a rough estimate, not a precise measurement — useful for noticing a big shift, not for meaningful day-to-day comparison.
3. Let how you actually feel carry more weight than the number on the app. If you feel rested and the app disagrees, trust how you feel.
4. If checking your sleep data first thing in the morning is becoming a source of dread rather than information, that's worth noticing — the data isn't required reading every day.
5. Use trackers to spot genuinely useful patterns — does a late workout, alcohol, or a stressful day show up consistently in your data — rather than to grade a single night.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the nightly "sleep score" as objective truth.** It's a composite built on relatively low-resolution data, not a clinical measurement.
- **Comparing sleep stage percentages night to night as if they were precise.** Different trackers frequently disagree with each other on the same person, same night — a sign the numbers aren't as exact as they look.
- **Waking up feeling fine, then feeling bad specifically because the app rated the night poorly.** This is the exact mechanism behind orthosomnia — the subjective feeling gets overridden by the device.
- **Chasing a perfect score.** There's no meaningful evidence that optimising every sub-metric on a consumer tracker produces better daytime function than simply sleeping consistently and long enough.
- **Abandoning genuinely useful trend-watching because of one bad reading.** The device is more useful over weeks than it is on any single night — don't throw out the pattern because of one outlier.

## What should change

Used this way, tracker data should become a background signal you check periodically, rather than a source of nightly anxiety — the shift is in how you use the data, not the data itself. If you've noticed dread or compulsive checking specifically because of tracker scores, stepping back from it for a couple of weeks is a reasonable, low-cost experiment.

## Your next step

If a tracker consistently shows fragmented sleep and you also feel poorly rested, that combination is worth paying attention to — the articles on caffeine timing, alcohol, and waking at 3am cover the most common, fixable causes before assuming something more serious is going on.
