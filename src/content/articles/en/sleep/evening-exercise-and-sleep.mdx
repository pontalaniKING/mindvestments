Train in the evening without worrying about it, including fairly intense sessions — just finish anything genuinely strenuous at least an hour before bed, and ideally two to four hours if you can manage it. The old blanket advice to avoid all evening exercise turns out not to hold up.

## Why this works

Sleep hygiene guidelines have discouraged evening exercise for decades, on a reasonable-sounding theory: exercise raises heart rate, body temperature, and stress hormones, all of which should work against falling asleep. It's a sensible hypothesis. It's also, according to the best current evidence, mostly wrong for the intensity and timing most people actually train at.

A 2025 study analysed roughly 4 million nights of real-world wearable data and found that exercise ending four or more hours before bedtime showed no meaningful association with sleep, regardless of intensity. A separate large-scale study of 150,000 nights found evening activity was actually linked to slightly earlier, not later, sleep onset. Multiple meta-analyses pooling controlled trials reach a similar conclusion: moderate evening exercise doesn't disrupt sleep, and even high-intensity evening exercise generally doesn't either, provided there's roughly an hour of buffer before bed.

The real caveat, and it is a genuine one, is the narrow window right before sleep. The same large dataset found that strenuous exercise ending within about an hour of bedtime was consistently linked to delayed sleep onset — up to 80 minutes later in some cases for sessions ending very close to bedtime — along with shorter total sleep and elevated nighttime heart rate. The effect scales with how close to bedtime and how intense the session was, not with "evening" as a blanket category. A moderate evening walk or an easy bike ride essentially never causes this. An all-out interval session finishing forty minutes before you try to sleep, more plausibly will.

## How to do it

1. Stop treating "evening" as the problem. The actual variable that matters is how close to bedtime a genuinely strenuous session ends.
2. For anything moderate — a normal strength session, an easy-to-moderate run, most group classes — don't worry about timing relative to bed at all, based on current evidence.
3. For something genuinely maximal or very high intensity, build in at least an hour of buffer, and treat two to four hours as the safer target if your schedule allows it.
4. If you only have time to train close to bedtime, a lower-intensity version of the same session is a reasonable trade rather than skipping it entirely.
5. Notice your own response over a couple of weeks — population-level findings describe an average, and a minority of people are more sensitive to late training than most.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping evening exercise entirely because of outdated sleep hygiene advice.** For most people at most training intensities, this is an unnecessary restriction with no real payoff.
- **Assuming intensity doesn't matter as long as it's "evening."** The disruption is concentrated in strenuous exercise finishing within about an hour of bed, not evening exercise generally.
- **Judging this from one bad night.** Individual variation exists — if a late hard session consistently disrupts your sleep specifically, that's useful personal data, not a reason to distrust the broader research.
- **Confusing this with the separate advice about bright light and screens in the evening.** A gym's lighting or a late-evening run outdoors is a minor consideration next to exercise timing itself.

## What should change

For moderate evening exercise, expect no change to sleep onset or quality — that's the actual finding, not a cautious hedge. For strenuous exercise, expect sleep to be minimally affected once there's an hour or more of buffer, with the effect becoming more noticeable the closer to bedtime a hard session runs.

## Your next step

If evening training is being avoided specifically to protect sleep, and that's the only reason, this is a genuinely low-cost thing to test for yourself over two weeks — training earlier isn't required by the evidence the way older guidelines implied.
