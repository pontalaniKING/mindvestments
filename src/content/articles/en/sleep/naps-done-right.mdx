If you're going to nap, keep it to about 20 minutes and finish before 3pm. Set an alarm rather than trusting yourself to wake up on time.

## Why this works

A nap's effect on you depends almost entirely on two things: how long it runs and how late in the day it happens, and both have a fairly precise mechanism behind them.

Length matters because of sleep architecture. In the first 20 minutes or so, you're mostly in light sleep, which is easy to wake from and tends to leave you feeling refreshed. Push past that and you start dropping into deep, slow-wave sleep — waking out of that stage produces sleep inertia, the groggy, disoriented feeling that can last twenty minutes or more and often leaves people feeling worse than before they napped, not better.

Timing matters because of sleep pressure, the build-up of adenosine across the day that makes you feel sleepy and eventually lets you fall asleep at night. A short nap barely dents it. A late-afternoon or early-evening nap, especially a longer one, can drain enough of that accumulated pressure that falling asleep at the normal bedtime becomes noticeably harder — you're borrowing from the same pool the night needs.

## How to do it

1. Set an alarm for 20 minutes before you lie down, not after — it's easy to underestimate how fast you'll actually fall asleep.
2. Nap before 3pm. Earlier is safer than later if you're unsure how it'll affect your night.
3. A short, sub-caffeine-half-life coffee right before a 20-minute nap — the so-called "caffeine nap" — genuinely works for some people: the caffeine takes about that long to kick in, so you wake up as it starts working.
4. Lying down somewhere reasonably dark and cool helps you actually fall asleep in a short window rather than spending half the nap just relaxing.
5. If you wake up groggy despite keeping it short, give it ten to fifteen minutes before judging how you feel — some inertia even from light sleep is normal and passes quickly.

## Common mistakes

- **"I'll just close my eyes for a bit" with no alarm.** This is how a 20-minute nap becomes a 90-minute one.
- **Napping because you're bored, not because you're actually sleepy.** If it's not real sleep pressure, a short walk does more for alertness than lying down.
- **A long nap to "catch up" after a bad night.** It feels logical and usually backfires — it eases today's tiredness at the cost of tonight's sleep, extending the problem instead of fixing it.
- **Napping late because it was the only free time.** The nap's usefulness depends on timing as much as length; a late one is a worse trade even if it's shorter.
- **Judging a nap immediately on waking.** The first few minutes of grogginess after any nap, even a short one, isn't a reliable read on whether it helped.

## What should change

A well-timed 20-minute nap should leave you measurably more alert within the same afternoon, without making it any harder to fall asleep at your normal bedtime that night. If a nap is consistently followed by a slow, worse-than-before afternoon, it likely ran too long or too late — shorten it or move it earlier before giving up on napping altogether.

## Your next step

If afternoon energy is the actual problem and naps aren't practical, ten minutes of daylight and a short walk do a lot of the same job without touching sleep pressure at all.
